## 1. Premise

Miss Marilyn ("Miss M") is a small rescue cat with a big curious heart. In each book she goes on
one everyday adventure that feels huge to a little kid (a plane ride, a visit to her grandparents,
the first day of school). Something about it feels new or a bit wobbly, she works through it with
her family and her best friend Zoey, she sneezes at least once ("Achoo!"), and by the end she has
learned one small, true thing about growing up.

The books star a real cat. The last page of every book shows a real photo of Miss Marilyn, so
kids learn that the cat in the story is a real cat who lives with a real family.

## 2. Audience

- **Listeners:** children ages 2–6. A 2-year-old follows the pictures, the sneeze and the
  refrain; a 5-year-old follows the story and the lesson.
- **Readers:** parents, grandparents and caregivers reading aloud, often at bedtime, often for
  the tenth time. The text has to be pleasant to say out loud on the tenth read.
- **Buyers:** parents and gift-givers, mostly shopping because a real event is coming up (a trip,
  a new school, a visit). The adventure is the reason they buy; the lesson is the reason they keep it.

## 3. Book format

- 8.5 x 8.5 in, 24 pages (KDP's minimum), premium colour, paperback first. Shorter on purpose
  (decided 2026-10-30): 10 story spreads, story text on 11–15 pages, about 250–400 words in all.
- One adventure, one lesson per book. No sub-plots.
- Under 50 words per spread (two facing pages). Most spreads should be 20–40 words. Hard cap:
  a spread over 50 words fails QC.
- Words a three-year-old knows, or can understand from the picture. One "big word" per book is
  fine if the picture explains it (for example "runway").
- Short sentences. Present or simple past tense; pick one per book and keep it.

### Beat map for the 10 story spreads

1. **Spread 1 — The news.** Someone tells Miss M about the adventure. She is excited and
   a little unsure. Introduce the refrain.
2. **Spread 2 — Getting ready.** Packing, practising, asking questions. First "Achoo!" usually
   lands here (small, funny).
3. **Spreads 3–6 — The adventure.** Two or three small moments, each a little bigger than the
   last. One moment of gentle worry (never danger). Zoey or a family member helps.
4. **Spreads 7–8 — The turn.** Miss M tries the thing she was unsure about. The lesson happens
   here through what she does, not through a speech.
5. **Spreads 9–10 — Back home / wind down.** Calm, warm, bedtime-friendly. The lesson in one
   short line a parent can repeat later. Final refrain or final sneeze.

## 4. The running gag: "Achoo!"

Miss M has a sneeze that always arrives at the most surprising moment. It is the series
signature: kids wait for it, shout it, and fake-sneeze along.

**How it works**

- **Set-up line, then the sneeze.** The sneeze is always announced by her nose: a set-up line the
  reader can slow down on, then a page turn or a beat, then "Achoo!"
  - Series set-up line (*draft — Clara to confirm*): **"Her nose went tickle... tickle... tickle..."**
  - Then: **"ACHOO!"** (always capitals, always its own line, always with the exclamation mark)
- **At least once per book.** Two or three is better. Never more than four, or it stops being a
  surprise.
- **Escalate.** The first sneeze is small and silly. The last one is the biggest and lands at
  the turn or the ending.
- **The sneeze causes a small, harmless, funny result.** Examples of variations:
  - *The scatter:* blows a pile of something everywhere (leaves, flour, confetti, paper napkins).
  - *The surprise helper:* the sneeze accidentally solves the problem (blows a stuck kite free,
    rings a bell that calls everyone to lunch, startles a shy new friend into giggling).
  - *The false alarm:* the nose tickles, everyone braces... and nothing happens. Then one page
    later, when nobody expects it: "ACHOO!"
  - *The echo:* someone else sneezes too (Poppy, Zoey, a new friend), and they laugh together.
  - *The bless-you chorus:* the family answers together with a line kids can join in on
    (*draft:* "Bless you, Miss M!").
- **Triggers are everyday and harmless:** a flower, a feather, dust, pepper in Nonna's kitchen,
  a fluffy cloud of pillow fluff, a sunbeam.

**Rules**

- The sneeze is never a sign of being sick. No germs, no tissues-and-medicine scenes, no
  "she's getting a cold".
- Nothing gross: no drips, no spray drawn in the art beyond a cartoon puff.
- Nobody is hurt or embarrassed in a mean way. Everyone laughs *with* Miss M.
- The art shows the sneeze as a clear, readable moment: eyes squeezed shut, small puff lines,
  things flying. The word "ACHOO!" is set by the Builder as text, never drawn into the art.

## 5. Refrain

Each book has **one refrain** of 3–8 words that repeats 3–5 times and changes slightly at the
end. Kids should be able to say it with you by the second read.

- Example (plane book): "Up, up, up we go!" → last time: "Down, down, home we go."
- Example (school book): "One small step, then one step more."
- The refrain is separate from the sneeze set-up line. Both can appear in the same book.

## 8. Tone and writing rules

**Tone:** warm, cozy, gently funny. A safe world where small things feel big and always turn
out fine. The lesson is shown, not preached.

**Do**
- Write for the ear: rhythm, repetition, a little sound play (whoosh, pitter-pat, beep).
- Name feelings simply: excited, nervous, wobbly, proud, sleepy.
- Let Miss M solve the problem herself, with help, not have it solved for her.
- Keep each spread to one picture-able moment.
- End calm enough for bedtime.
- Leave room for the art: if the picture shows it, the text doesn't need to say it.

**Don't**
- No peril beyond gentle worry. Nobody gets lost for long, hurt, sick, or in danger.
- No scary villains, monsters, bullies or mean characters. Conflict comes from new situations
  and feelings, not from someone bad.
- No brands, products, real places, real people, songs or TV shows.
- No imitation of existing books or characters. In particular, stay clearly away from other
  famous picture-book cats (Pete the Cat, Splat the Cat, Bad Kitty, Skippyjon Jones, Pusheen,
  Hello Kitty) and from the structure and phrasing of well-known books (cause-and-effect chains
  like "If You Give a Mouse a Cookie", "Llama Llama" rhyme patterns, Daniel Tiger strategy songs).
- No rhyming unless the whole book rhymes well; forced rhyme is worse than none.
- No sarcasm, put-downs, potty humour, or jokes only adults get.
- No lecturing lines like "And that's why we should always...". The final lesson line is in
  Miss M's or a family member's voice and is under 12 words.
- No gendered or cultural stereotypes, no food shaming, no screen-time moralising.
